## Position on the periodic table

- **Symbol**: Li
- **Atomic number** ($Z$): 3
- **Atomic weight** ($A_r$): 6.94 (range [6.938, 6.997])
- **Group**: 1 (alkali metals)
- **Period**: 2

This makes lithium:
- The **third element** on the periodic table, after hydrogen and helium.
- The **first alkali metal** (the lightest of the Group 1 family: Li, Na, K,
  Rb, Cs, Fr).
- The **lightest metal** of all (sometimes "lightest solid metal" — H₂ is
  technically a metallic solid only at extreme pressures).

## Electron configuration

Lithium has 3 electrons. They fill orbitals as:

$$ 1s^2 \ 2s^1 $$

That is: two electrons in the inner $1s$ shell (a "core" pair), one
electron in the outer $2s$ shell (the "valence" electron).

This single valence electron is the source of all of lithium's chemistry.

## Key properties

| Property | Value | Comment |
|----------|-------|---------|
| Density (metal, 25 °C) | 0.534 g/cm³ | Lowest density of any solid element under STP |
| Melting point | 180.5 °C | Lowest of any alkali metal |
| Boiling point | 1330 °C | Surprisingly high — strong metallic bonding for an alkali |
| First ionization energy | 5.39 eV | Low (easy to ionize), highest among alkalis |
| Second ionization energy | 75.6 eV | Huge (would have to dig into core 1s) |
| Electronegativity (Pauling) | 0.98 | Low — happy to give up electron |
| Standard reduction potential E°(Li⁺/Li) | –3.04 V | Most negative of any common metal |
| Atomic radius | 1.52 Å | Small — second-period element |
| Ionic radius (Li⁺, 6-coord) | 0.76 Å | Smallest of common +1 cations |
| Hardness | Cuts with a knife | Very soft metal |
| Reaction with water | Vigorous, gives Li⁺, OH⁻, H₂ | But less violent than Na, K, Rb, Cs |

## Stable isotopes

| Isotope | Atomic mass (u) | Natural abundance | Nuclear spin |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|--------------|
| Li-6 (³Li⁶) | 6.01512 | 7.59% | 1 |
| Li-7 (³Li⁷) | 7.01600 | 92.41% | 3/2 |

Both are stable. All other lithium isotopes (Li-3 through Li-12) are
radioactive with half-lives of milliseconds or less.

The 7.59% / 92.41% ratio is the natural starting point. Every isotope-
separation method we'll discuss takes this ratio as input and tries to
push it toward higher Li-6 fractions (or higher Li-7 fractions, depending
on application).

## Why lithium is "weird" — the second-period anomaly

Lithium sits at the top of Group 1. It's smaller than the other alkalis
(by a lot), and several of its behaviors deviate from the smooth trends
that hold for Na, K, Rb, Cs:

1. **Higher charge density**. With Li⁺ at 0.76 Å (vs. Na⁺ at 1.02 Å, K⁺
   at 1.38 Å), the +1 charge of Li⁺ is concentrated into a much smaller
   volume. Charge density (~ q/r) is the variable that drives:
   - Stronger hydration shell (Li⁺'s hydration enthalpy is ~–520 kJ/mol;
     Cs⁺ is only –260 kJ/mol).
   - Stronger ion-dipole interactions in non-aqueous solvents.
   - Stronger polarization of nearby anions, leading to more covalent
     character in Li compounds.
2. **Diagonal relationship with magnesium**. Li sits diagonally adjacent
   to Mg on the periodic table; Li and Mg share several anomalous
   properties (e.g., both react slowly with water at room temp; both form
   relatively covalent halides; both Li and Mg, unusually for their groups,
   form nitrides directly with N₂; the hydroxides Li(OH) and Mg(OH)₂ are
   thermally decomposable to oxides where the heavier alkali hydroxides
   are not).
3. **Lithium reactivity with water is mild for an alkali metal**. Na
   skitters across the surface and ignites; K bursts into flames; Rb and Cs
   explode. Lithium just fizzes. Why? It floats (lowest density) so it's
   less efficient at heat-driven runaway, and its small ion has a
   relatively *high* ionization energy among the alkalis (5.39 eV vs.
   3.89 eV for Cs).
4. **Highest reducing power per gram of any solid metal**. The standard
   reduction potential of Li⁺/Li is –3.04 V, more negative than Na⁺/Na
   (–2.71 V) or K⁺/K (–2.93 V). Combined with low atomic mass, this gives
   lithium the highest specific energy of any metal anode — the *reason*
   lithium-ion batteries dominate.
5. **High specific heat for a metal**. Liquid lithium is used as a heat
   transfer fluid in some advanced reactor concepts.

## Where lithium is found in nature

Lithium is the 33rd most abundant element in Earth's crust at ~20 ppm —
rarer than copper, comparable to lead. It is found in:

| Source | Form | Where |
|--------|------|-------|
| **Brines** | Dissolved Li⁺ in salt-rich groundwater | Salars of Chile, Bolivia, Argentina (the "Lithium Triangle"); China's Qaidam Basin; Salton Sea (USA); Smackover Formation (USA) |
| **Hard-rock pegmatites** | Spodumene (LiAlSi₂O₆), petalite (LiAlSi₄O₁₀), lepidolite | Greenbushes (Australia), North Carolina (USA), Manono (DRC) |
| **Sedimentary clays** | Lithium adsorbed in hectorite-type clays | McDermitt Caldera / Thacker Pass (USA, Nevada/Oregon) |
| **Geothermal brines** | Heated, deep-source brines | Cornwall (UK), Cesano (Italy), Salton Sea (USA) |
| **Sea water** | ~0.17 ppm Li⁺ | Vast resource, but currently uneconomical |

For ordinary lithium-as-a-commodity (batteries, ceramics, lubricant
greases, glass, mood-stabilizing pharmaceuticals — yes, the lithium in
psychiatric medication is just regular lithium carbonate), the supply
chain is "natural-abundance lithium." No isotope separation involved.

For Li-6-enriched lithium (fusion, nuclear, niche scientific), supply is
*almost entirely* government / strategic. Commercial markets for enriched
Li-6 barely exist. This is one of the reasons the lithium-isotope-
separation literature is suddenly so active in the 2020s — fusion
companies need it, and there's no commercial supplier.

## Industrial relevance — natural lithium vs. enriched lithium

Important distinction. Don't conflate them.

### Natural lithium (~7.59% Li-6, ~92.41% Li-7) — the commodity

- **Lithium-ion batteries** — the dominant use. Mobile phones, EVs, grid
  storage. The lithium ion shuttles between cathode (e.g., LiFePO₄) and
  anode (e.g., graphite intercalation) to store and release charge.
  Isotope identity doesn't matter; performance is identical.
- **Greases** — lithium-soap-based greases work in extreme temperatures.
- **Glass and ceramics** — lithium oxide reduces melting point and improves
  thermal-shock resistance.
- **Pharmaceuticals** — lithium carbonate / lithium citrate as mood
  stabilizers (bipolar disorder treatment).
- **Aluminum alloys** — Li-Al alloys are extremely lightweight, used in
  aerospace.
- **Air conditioning and dehumidification** — lithium bromide and lithium
  chloride solutions for desiccant systems.

### Li-6 enriched lithium — the strategic material

- **Fusion energy** — Li-6 is consumed by the breeding reaction
  $\text{Li-6} + n \rightarrow \text{T} + \alpha + 4.78\ \text{MeV}$
  to produce tritium fuel for D-T fusion. Every credible fusion reactor
  concept (ITER, DEMO, ARC, SPARC, JET, future commercial fusion
  reactors) needs enriched Li-6 in its breeding blanket. Enrichment
  targets: typically 30–90% Li-6.
- **Nuclear weapons (historical)** — Li-6-D (lithium-6 deuteride) was the
  fusion fuel in thermonuclear weapons. The original COLEX programs at
  Oak Ridge (1953-1963) and at Sarov in the USSR were built for this
  purpose.
- **Neutron detection** — Li-6 has a large thermal-neutron capture cross
  section and emits charged particles that scintillator detectors can
  read. Used in scientific neutron facilities.
- **Some research applications** in NMR (Li-6 has spin 1, useful for
  certain solid-state experiments).

### Li-7 enriched lithium — also strategic, less famous

- **Nuclear fission reactors using molten salt** (e.g., the FLiBe
  coolant: ⁷LiF + BeF₂). Li-7 is preferred because Li-6's high
  thermal-neutron-capture cross section would generate tritium (which is
  problematic in a fission system).
- **Pressurized water reactor pH control**. The coolant in a PWR is
  treated with LiOH to maintain alkaline pH; Li-7 is preferred to avoid
  tritium production from neutron-thermalized cooling water.

So both isotopes have separate strategic uses. The COLEX byproduct of
Li-6 enrichment is depleted Li-7, also valuable.
