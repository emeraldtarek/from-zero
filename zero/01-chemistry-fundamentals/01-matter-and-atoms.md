# 01 — Matter and Atoms

## Prerequisite check

None. This is the floor.

## Rigorous statement

**Matter** is anything that has *mass* and *occupies volume*. It comes in
discrete units called **atoms**. Every macroscopic substance you have ever
touched is an arrangement of roughly $10^{22}$ to $10^{24}$ atoms.

*Mass* (kg) is a measure of how much "stuff" something has — equivalently, how
much it resists being accelerated by a force ($F = ma$, Newton). It is not the
same thing as *weight*, which is the gravitational force *on* a mass
($W = mg$). A 1 kg object has the same mass on Earth as on the Moon; its
weight differs by a factor of ~6.

The defining claim of chemistry — what physicists made physical, what
chemistry is built on — is the **atomic hypothesis**: matter is not infinitely
divisible. There exists a smallest unit of every chemical element, the
**atom**, below which the substance loses its identity.

## Plain-English unpacking

Take a glass of water. Pour out half of it — still water. Pour out half of
*that* — still water. Keep going. If you could keep halving long enough, you'd
eventually be holding a **single molecule** of water (H₂O). Halve that, and
it's no longer water — it's hydrogen and oxygen atoms. Halve an atom and you
no longer have hydrogen or oxygen, you have a nucleus and some electrons.

So matter is **discrete**. There are unit bricks, and the bricks have an
identity that the bulk substance inherits. That's the whole game.

## Concrete picture

A water molecule is roughly **0.3 nanometers** across — that is
$3 \times 10^{-10}$ m, three ten-billionths of a meter. To see one, you'd
need to magnify it by ~10 million.

If you scaled a water molecule up to the size of a marble (1 cm), then on the
same scale a single drop of water would be the size of the Earth. The drop
contains roughly $1.7 \times 10^{21}$ molecules — a number larger than the
estimated number of stars in the observable universe.

You don't see atoms not because they're "imaginary," but because **visible
light has wavelengths of 400–700 nm** — about 1000× larger than an atom. You
can't resolve something smaller than the wavelength you're using to look at
it. We see atoms today with electron microscopes (electrons have de Broglie
wavelengths thousands of times shorter than visible light) and scanning
tunneling microscopes (which exploit quantum tunneling, not optical imaging
at all).

## Worked example / specific data

A few load-bearing numbers you should hold in your head:

| Quantity | Value | Why it matters |
|----------|-------|----------------|
| Atomic radius (typical) | ~10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.1 nm = 1 Å (ångström) | Sets the scale of all of chemistry |
| Nuclear radius | ~10⁻¹⁵ m = 1 fm (femtometer) | Atom is 100,000× wider than its nucleus |
| Avogadro's number $N_A$ | $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ mol⁻¹ | The "exchange rate" between atomic and macroscopic scales |
| Proton mass | $1.673 \times 10^{-27}$ kg | Sets the unit of atomic mass |
| 1 mole of water (18 g) | $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ molecules | One tablespoon of water has ~10²⁴ molecules |

**The mole** is just a counting unit, the way a "dozen" is. A dozen eggs is 12
eggs; a mole of atoms is $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ atoms. Why this weird number?
Because 12 grams of carbon-12 contains exactly that many carbon atoms. It was
chosen so that the mass of one mole of atoms (in grams) equals the atomic
mass (in atomic mass units). It's a bridge between the atomic scale and the
laboratory scale.

## How we *know* atoms exist

You should never accept "atoms exist" as an article of faith. The evidence is
overwhelming and historically rich:

1. **Dalton (1803)**: Discovered that elements combine in fixed *integer*
   mass ratios when forming compounds. (Law of multiple proportions.) Discrete
   building blocks are the simplest explanation.
2. **Brownian motion (Einstein, 1905)**: A pollen grain in water jitters
   because it's being pelted by *individual* water molecules. Einstein's
   mathematical analysis of this jitter let Perrin (1908) experimentally
   determine $N_A$ and won Perrin the 1926 Nobel Prize. This was the proof
   that ended any serious resistance to atomism.
3. **Cathode rays / Thomson (1897)**: Discovered the electron, a *piece* of
   the atom — implying atoms have substructure.
4. **Rutherford gold-foil experiment (1909)**: Alpha particles fired at a
   gold foil mostly passed straight through, but ~1 in 8000 bounced back.
   Conclusion: atoms have a tiny dense **nucleus** and are mostly empty space.
5. **Scanning tunneling microscopy (Binnig & Rohrer, 1981)**: We can
   *literally* image individual atoms now. Nobel 1986.

You can put your hands on atoms today via STM images of, say, an iron atom
sitting on copper. The "atomic hypothesis" is the experimental bedrock of
modern chemistry and condensed-matter physics.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Atoms are tiny solid balls."** No. Atoms are mostly empty space, with
  a tiny dense nucleus (positive charge) and a diffuse cloud of electrons
  (negative charge) around it. The "ball" picture is fine for cartoons but
  will mislead you the moment you try to do real chemistry.
- **"Atoms can't be split."** They can; that's literally what nuclear
  fission is. The Greek root *atomos* means "uncuttable," but the word stuck
  even after we discovered atoms have parts.
- **"Mass and weight are the same thing."** They're not. Mass is intrinsic;
  weight depends on the local gravitational field. In chemistry we always
  mean mass.
- **"Avogadro's number is special."** It's a definition, not a discovery.
  It's the number of carbon-12 atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12. Pick a
  different reference mass and you'd get a different "Avogadro number."

## Self-check questions

1. If matter is mostly empty space, why doesn't your hand pass through a
   table? (Hint: think about which forces are at play, not just which
   particles are present.)
2. What does it mean that 1 mole of carbon weighs 12 grams? Where does the
   "12" come from?
3. Why was Brownian motion considered the *proof* of atomic theory rather
   than just suggestive evidence?
4. If atoms have radii of ~10⁻¹⁰ m and humans are ~1 m tall, how many
   "atom-widths" tall are you? Compute it. Hold the answer in your head as a
   scale calibration.
5. The mass of a single water molecule is ~3 × 10⁻²⁶ kg. About how many
   water molecules are in a 1-liter bottle? (Density of water = 1000 kg/m³.)
   Show your steps.
