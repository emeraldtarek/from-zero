## Coagulation and flocculation

### Principle
Natural waters contain colloidal particles (1 nm–1 µm) that are too small
to settle out by gravity alone. They stay in suspension because their
**negative surface charge** (zeta potential, typically −20 to −40 mV)
keeps them mutually repulsive. **Coagulation** neutralizes the surface
charge; **flocculation** then aggregates the destabilized particles into
larger flocs that *can* settle.

### Mechanism (DLVO theory + practice)
Four destabilization paths:
1. **Double-layer compression**: high ionic strength compresses each
   particle's electrical double layer, reducing inter-particle repulsion.
2. **Adsorption / charge neutralization**: cationic coagulants (Al³⁺,
   Fe³⁺) adsorb to particle surfaces and neutralize their charge.
3. **Sweep / enmeshment flocculation**: metal hydroxide precipitates
   (Al(OH)₃, Fe(OH)₃) form a voluminous floc that physically entraps
   colloids.
4. **Polymer bridging**: long-chain polymers adsorb onto multiple
   particles, knitting them together.

### Typical coagulants
- **Alum** (aluminum sulfate, Al₂(SO₄)₃·18H₂O): the workhorse. Optimal
  pH 6–8.
- **Ferric chloride or ferric sulfate**: broader pH range, denser floc.
- **Polyaluminum chloride (PACl)**: pre-hydrolyzed, low-temperature
  performance.
- **Cationic polymers**: as primary or supplementary coagulants.

### Mixing intensities
- Rapid mix (coagulation): velocity gradient G ~700–1000 s⁻¹, contact
  time 1–2 min.
- Slow mix (flocculation): G ~20–80 s⁻¹, contact time 20–40 min. Too
  much shear breaks the flocs.

### Removes
Turbidity, natural organic matter (NOM), microorganisms (in
combination with later disinfection), colloidal metals.

### Limitations
Sludge production, pH-sensitive, doesn't remove dissolved species.

## Sedimentation / clarification

### Principle
Gravity settling. Stokes' law gives the terminal settling velocity of a
spherical particle:

$$ v_s = \frac{(\rho_p - \rho_w) g d_p^2}{18 \mu} $$

where $\rho_p, \rho_w$ are particle and water densities, $d_p$ is particle
diameter, $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity. Big and dense → settles fast; small
or low-density → settles slow.

### Industrial forms
- **Conventional clarifier**: horizontal flow, surface overflow rate (SOR)
  20–40 m/d.
- **Lamella plates / tube settlers**: inclined surfaces, increase
  effective settling area, reduce footprint 4–8×.
- **Dissolved air flotation (DAF)**: micro-bubbles attach to low-density
  particles (algae, oil), float to surface, get skimmed.

### Removes
Coagulated flocs, large suspended solids.

### Byproduct
Sludge (95–99% water) requiring thickening and dewatering.

## Filtration

### Rapid sand filter (RSF)
- Sand 0.5–1.5 mm, bed depth 0.6–0.9 m, filtration rate 5–15 m/h.
- Removes: post-coagulation flocs, residual turbidity, microbes.
- Backwashed when headloss exceeds limit (~2.5–3 m).
- Backwash water: 2–5% of treated flow.

### Multimedia filter
- Anthracite (top, coarse) → sand → garnet (bottom, fine).
- Allows more solids storage per cycle.

### Slow sand filter (SSF)
- Filtration rate 0.1–0.3 m/h (very slow).
- A biological mat ("Schmutzdecke") on the surface provides pathogen and
  organic removal.
- No coagulation needed; works for low-turbidity feeds.
- Simple, but needs lots of land.

### Granular activated carbon (GAC)
- Adsorbs NOM, trace organics, taste-and-odor compounds, some pesticides.
- BET surface area: 500–1,500 m²/g.
- Empty Bed Contact Time (EBCT): 5–20 min.
- Regenerated by thermal/steam treatment when breakthrough is reached.

### Limitation
Filtration removes particulates and adsorbable organics. It does **not**
remove dissolved ions. For Li⁺ in solution, filtration is irrelevant
(other than as pretreatment to protect downstream ion-handling
equipment).

## Disinfection

You'll see disinfection mentioned because chlorination is the canonical
"is the water safe to drink?" step. For the lithium project it's mostly
irrelevant — Li-6 plants don't drink their feed — but understand the
vocabulary.

The **CT concept**: disinfection efficacy = concentration × contact time
(mg·min/L). Bigger CT, more log-inactivation of pathogens.

| Agent | Mechanism | Trade-offs |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **Chlorine (Cl₂)** | Oxidizes membranes, enzymes | Cheap, residual; forms DBPs (THMs, HAAs) by reaction with NOM |
| **Chloramine (NH₂Cl)** | Like chlorine, more stable | Lower DBPs; less effective for Cryptosporidium; can form NDMA |
| **Ozone (O₃)** | Powerful oxidant (E° = +2.07 V) | Excellent on Cryptosporidium; no residual; produces bromate |
| **UV** | Damages microbe DNA | No chemicals, no residual; doesn't remove anything else |
| **Peracetic acid** | Oxidizes | Used in wastewater niche applications |

## Aeration / stripping

Volatile contaminants (radon, hydrogen sulfide, volatile organics) can be
driven out of solution by contact with air. Henry's law sets the
gas-liquid partition. Used as a polishing step in some drinking-water
trains and in groundwater remediation.

## Adsorption (the non-ion-exchange kind)

**Activated carbon, zeolites, resins for organics** rather than ions:

- Activated carbon adsorbs organic micropollutants via van der Waals and
  hydrophobic interactions.
- Zeolites (microporous aluminosilicates) adsorb based on size and
  charge. *Some specialized zeolites are used as Li sorbents in
  brine recovery* — that's a bridge to the next file.
- Synthetic adsorbent resins (e.g., for arsenic, perchlorate) target
  specific ions but operate via adsorption rather than classical ion
  exchange.

## Distillation

The classical separation — boil the water, condense the vapor, leave the
non-volatile salts behind. Energy-intensive but conceptually simple.

| Method | Energy | Use case |
|--------|--------|----------|
| **Multi-stage flash (MSF)** | 13.5–25.5 kWh/m³ | Old desalination workhorse, especially Middle East |
| **Multi-effect distillation (MED)** | <1 kWh/m³ electrical (plus thermal energy) | More efficient than MSF |
| **Mechanical vapor compression (MVC)** | 6–13 kWh/m³ | Niche industrial, high-purity uses |

Distillation matters for the lithium project as a *generic separation
principle*: vapor and liquid have different compositions at equilibrium,
and you can drive a separation by repeated evaporation and condensation.
**Liquid lithium can be distilled** for isotope work — Li-6 and Li-7 have
slightly different vapor pressures (heavier isotope, lower vapor pressure)
yielding a small per-stage isotope-separation factor. Not industrial
today for Li, but conceptually relevant.

## Chemical precipitation

Force a target ion into an insoluble compound by adjusting chemistry,
then filter out the solid.

- **Lime softening**: add Ca(OH)₂ to precipitate CaCO₃ and Mg(OH)₂.
- **Sulfide precipitation**: add S²⁻ to precipitate heavy metals (HgS,
  PbS, CuS, etc.).
- **Lithium carbonate precipitation**: the *final* step in most
  brine-based Li recovery: concentrate Li⁺ to ~1–6%, then add Na₂CO₃ to
  precipitate Li₂CO₃, which is then filtered, washed, and dried as the
  product.

For the lithium project, **Li₂CO₃ precipitation is universal** — it's how
natural-abundance Li gets converted from a dissolved ion into a sellable
solid, and it's also where many isotope-separation product streams end
up after the actual fractionation step.

## Putting it all together: a typical brine-to-lithium plant

A simplified, generic flow for converting a salt-flat brine into
battery-grade Li₂CO₃ (no isotope separation; just element-level recovery):

```
Salar brine (Li 100–2000 mg/L, TDS 100,000–300,000 mg/L)
   │
   ▼
[Solar evaporation ponds]   — concentrate Li to 1–6%; days to months
   │
   ▼
[Boron extraction]          — solvent extraction with organic solvents
   │
   ▼
[Mg/Ca removal]             — chemical precipitation with lime + soda ash
   │
   ▼
[Li sorbent column or Li/Mg-selective ED]   — concentrate and purify Li⁺
   │
   ▼
[Polishing]                 — ion exchange to remove residual Mg, Ca
   │
   ▼
[Carbonate precipitation]   — add Na₂CO₃ → Li₂CO₃(s)
   │
   ▼
[Filter, wash, dry]         — battery-grade Li₂CO₃ product
```

You can see most of this file's vocabulary in action in those flow steps.
You can also see why the next file (ion handling) is the technical core —
the steps marked "Li sorbent column," "Mg/Ca removal," and "polishing"
are the actual *separations*; the rest is feed prep.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Water treatment is just chemistry."** It's *process engineering*:
  the equations are mass balance and unit-operation kinetics; the
  chemistry feeds in as parameters.
- **"All separation is purification."** No — *fractionation* (splitting
  one feed into two streams of different composition) and *purification*
  (removing contaminants from one feed) are subtly different framings.
  Isotope separation is fractionation; drinking-water treatment is
  purification.
- **"More steps = better water."** Each step has costs (chemicals,
  energy, byproducts). Real designs aim for the *minimum* number of
  unit operations that meet the spec.
- **"Distillation is obsolete."** Still dominant in multi-stage flash
  desalination plants in the Middle East, and still the conceptual model
  for many modern separations (gas-liquid stripping, vapor compression
  evaporators in ZLD).

## Self-check questions

1. What is the difference between coagulation and flocculation? Why are
   both needed?
2. Stokes' law says settling velocity $\propto d_p^2$. If you double a
   particle's diameter, how much faster does it settle? If you halve it?
3. The Schmutzdecke is what makes slow sand filtration work without
   coagulants. What is it, mechanistically?
4. Why does ozone produce a more aggressive disinfection than chlorine,
   yet chlorine remains the dominant US disinfectant?
5. Lithium carbonate precipitation is the *final* step in most lithium-
   recovery flows. Write the precipitation reaction
   (2 Li⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → Li₂CO₃) and explain in one sentence why this works
   (think solubility, hint: Li₂CO₃ is sparingly soluble).
